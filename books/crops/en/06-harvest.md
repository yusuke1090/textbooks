# 6. From harvest to the dinner table

## Harvest time

- The guide is an **accumulated temperature of about 1,000 °C** from heading, or about 40–45 days after heading
- A guide you can see in the field: when **80–90% of the grains have turned yellow** (a little green is left on the few grains at the base of the ear)
- Too early, and green rice (immature grains) gets mixed in and yield falls. Too late, and the rice cracks (fissured grains) or loses its color, and the grade drops
- Rice is not harvested when it is wet from rain or morning dew. If the grains hold a lot of water, drying them costs more fuel

## Harvesting

![A head-feeding combine. It cuts, threshes and chops the straw, all in one machine](images/combine-rice.jpg)

- Almost all rice harvesting today uses **head-feeding combines** (a machine born in Japan that passes only the ear part of the rice through the threshing unit). 4–6-row machines are common
- The harvested grain is held in the combine's tank, moved into a container on a truck, and taken to a drying facility

![Moving grain from the combine into the truck's container](images/combine-truck.jpg)

- In the past, harvested rice was tied into bundles and hung on wooden or bamboo racks to dry in the sun (***hazakake***, *hasagake*). Some farms still do this for specialty rice

![*Hazakake*. Harvested rice dries in the sun for about 2–3 weeks](images/hazakake.jpg)

## Drying, hulling and sorting

| Task | Machine | What it does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Drying** | Recirculating dryer | Slowly lowers the moisture from 20–25% at harvest to **about 15%** (14.5–15.0%). Drying too fast cracks the rice |
| **Hulling** | Huller | Removes the hulls to make brown rice. The hulls are used for compost, bedding and fuel |
| **Sorting** | Grader (sieve) | Removes small and broken grains (**screenings**, *kuzumai*) through a mesh (**1.80–1.90 mm** is usual) |
| **Color sorting** | Optical color sorter | A camera looks at each grain and blows out pecky black grains and foreign matter with air |

- Individual farmers either own their own dryer and huller, or take the rice to JA's **country elevators** (large drying and storage facilities) or **rice centers**
- Brown rice is put into 30 kg paper bags or flexible containers (large 1 t bags) and stored in low-temperature warehouses (15 °C or below)
- National statistics count the harvest with a 1.70 mm sieve. Counted with the 1.80–1.90 mm that farmers usually use, the 2025 crop was about 7.18 million tonnes, about 300,000 tonnes less than the statistics

## Polishing

![A coin-operated rice polisher. Put in brown rice and get white rice on the spot](images/rice-polisher.jpg)

- Brown rice is ground in a polishing machine to remove the bran and germ, making white rice. About 90% of the brown rice becomes white rice (the milling yield)
- After polishing, rice gradually oxidizes and loses flavor. The fresher the polishing date, the better it tastes
- Brown rice has plenty of nutrients in the bran (vitamin B1 and dietary fiber). There is also "germ rice" that keeps the germ, and "partly polished rice" with only some of the bran removed

![Brown rice](images/brown-rice.jpg)

## Rice inspection and grades

- Brown rice for shipping is divided into **first grade, second grade, third grade and out of grade** by a registered inspection body (agricultural product inspection)

| Item | First-grade standard (non-glutinous brown rice) |
| --- | --- |
| Share of whole, sound grains | 70% or more |
| Moisture | 15.0% or less |
| Colored grains (pecky rice and so on) | 0.1% or less |
| Damaged grains, dead grains, other grains and so on | Below each upper limit |

- One grade lower means a price several hundred to about a thousand yen lower per 60 kg. Chalky immature grains from heat damage and pecky rice from stink bugs are the main reasons grades drop
- Bags show the place of origin, variety and crop year (the **"three-point set"**). To sell rice as, say, "Niigata Koshihikari, 2025 crop", it needs an inspection or a record confirming those details
- Taste is judged separately from the grade: by protein content (lower is said to taste better), taste analyzers, and taste rankings (such as "Special A" from the Japan Grain Inspection Association)

![A 5 kg bag of rice. It shows the place of origin, variety, crop year and polishing date](images/rice-bag.jpg)

## How rice is sold, and its price

- Main buyers
  - Shipping to **JA** (the agricultural cooperative): the farmer receives a **provisional payment** in autumn and settles up after the rice is sold
  - Selling to rice wholesalers and collectors
  - Selling directly to shops, restaurants, online or through hometown tax donations (**direct sales**)
- Price guides
  - **Negotiated transaction price**: the price at which collectors such as JA sell to wholesalers (per 60 kg of brown rice). The government publishes it every month
  - The shelf price at supermarkets (per 5 kg of white rice). The government publishes it every week
- Rice is protected by a high tariff on foreign rice (341 yen per kg)

## The history of acreage reduction

- After the war, rice was in short supply, but in the 1960s production rose, people started eating less, and **rice became surplus**
- From 1970, the government began **acreage reduction** (*gentan*, production adjustment), allocating paddy areas to cut the amount of rice grown. Farmers who grew wheat, soybeans or feed crops instead of rice received subsidies (**crop conversion**, *tensaku*)
- In **2018**, the government stopped allocating production amounts to each prefecture. Now it publishes a "demand outlook" and "production guide", and growing regions decide how much to grow based on them
- Even so, the subsidies for conversion (the **direct payment for paddy utilization**, 20,000–105,000 yen per 10 a) continued. From fiscal 2027, the government plans to change this into a system that supports **farmers who raise yields** of wheat, soybeans, feed crops and others, whether on paddies or upland fields (Chapter 7)

## The Reiwa rice crisis (2024–2025)

| When | What happened |
| --- | --- |
| Summer 2023 | Extreme heat increased chalky rice, reducing the amount that could be polished and eaten |
| Summer 2024 | Rice disappeared from shops. More foreign tourists, panic buying after earthquake warnings and the wait for the new crop all came at once |
| Autumn 2024–spring 2025 | Even after the new crop arrived, prices kept rising, and 5 kg at a supermarket went over 4,000 yen (about twice the previous year) |
| March–June 2025 | The government released **stockpiled rice** (rice it holds for emergencies). From May it stopped auctions and used direct contracts, and stockpiled rice at about 2,000 yen for 5 kg appeared in shops |
| Summer 2025 | The government announced a policy to "increase rice", and farmers planted more |
| Autumn 2025 | **The 2025 crop was about 7.47 million tonnes**, about 680,000 tonnes more than the year before and the highest since 2018 |
| October 2025 onward | Under a new cabinet, the policy went back to "production matched to demand", and the production guide for the 2026 crop was set at 7.11 million tonnes |
| 2026 | Stocks grew and prices fell. Private stocks in June were among the largest ever, and by October 5 kg at a supermarket had fallen to about 2,800 yen |

- This crisis showed how fragile the system had become: the government's outlook assumed "there is a little rice surplus every year", and after years of cutting production to the bare minimum, a small change was enough to cause a shortage
- On the other hand, if prices fall too far, there is a worry that more farmers will give up growing rice. Keeping **a price at which growers make a living and eaters can still afford to buy** is the challenge ahead
