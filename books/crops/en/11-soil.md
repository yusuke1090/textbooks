# 11. Soil and fertilizer

This chapter applies to both rice and upland crops.

## How soil is made up

![A soil profile. The black upper layer is rich in organic matter (humus), and crop roots grow into it](images/soil-profile.jpg)

### The three phases

Soil is made of three things (**the three phases of soil**).

| Phase | What it is | Guide (upland field) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Solid phase | Minerals (sand, silt, clay) and organic matter | About 50% |
| Liquid phase | Water | About 20–30% |
| Gas phase | Air | About 20–30% |

- Roots breathe too, so the soil needs **air**. Crops do not grow if there is only water (waterlogging) or only air (drought)
- Running large machines over a field many times packs the soil down (**soil compaction**), and air and water pass through less easily. Soil is especially easy to compact if you drive on a wet field

### Crumb structure

![Soil aggregates. Small particles stick together into lumps with many gaps](images/soil-aggregate.jpg)

- In soil where small particles are stuck together into small lumps (**aggregates**, or crumbs) by organic matter and the "glue" released by microbes,
  - water and air pass through the large gaps between the lumps (good **drainage**)
  - water is held in the small gaps inside the lumps (good **water retention**)
- This is ideal soil for crops: "drains well, yet also holds water well". Adding organic matter such as compost and not plowing too much helps aggregates form

### Humus and CEC

- The black substance made when fallen leaves, straw, compost and the like break down is called **humus**. It helps form aggregates and holds nutrients
- The soil's ability to hold onto fertilizer nutrients (positively charged ones such as potassium, calcium, magnesium and ammonium) is called **CEC** (cation exchange capacity). The more clay and humus, the larger the CEC, making a "long-lasting" soil from which fertilizer does not wash away easily
- **Andosols** (*kuroboku-do*, volcanic ash soils, common in Hokkaido, Kanto and Kyushu), which are common in Japanese fields, are soft soils rich in humus, but they hold **phosphate** so tightly that crops cannot use it. So more phosphate has to be applied

## pH

- How acidic the soil is (**pH**) greatly affects how easily nutrients dissolve and how well roots and microbes work
- Japan has a lot of rain, which washes away calcium and the like, so soils tend to become **acidic**. The pH is raised by spreading **lime** (calcium carbonate) or dolomitic lime (with magnesium)

| Crop | Right pH |
| --- | --- |
| Rice (paddy) | 5.5–6.5 (flooding naturally brings it close to neutral) |
| Wheat, barley | 6.0–7.0 (barley is weak against acidity) |
| Soybeans | 6.0–6.5 |
| Sugar beet | 6.5–7.5 (very weak against acidity) |
| Potatoes | 5.0–6.0 (common scab appears if it is high) |
| Buckwheat | 5.5–6.5 |

- In a rotation, the right pH differs by crop, so think about when and how much lime to spread (for example, hold back on lime just before potatoes)

## Nutrients in fertilizer

### The three main nutrients

| Nutrient | Main role | When short | When too much |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Nitrogen** (N) | Grows leaves and stems. The source of protein ("**leaf fertilizer**") | Leaves are yellow and small | Lodging, disease, more protein in rice so it tastes worse |
| **Phosphate** (P) | Grows roots, flowers and fruit. Handles energy ("**fruit fertilizer**") | Poor root growth, purplish leaves | Gets in the way of taking up other nutrients |
| **Potassium** (K) | Makes roots and stems sturdy and controls water movement ("**root fertilizer**") | Leaf edges die, plants fall over easily | Magnesium and calcium can no longer be taken up |

- Numbers on a fertilizer bag like "**8-8-8**" are the percentages of nitrogen, phosphate and potassium. A 20 kg bag contains 1.6 kg of each

### Other nutrients needed

- **Calcium** (Ca), **magnesium** (Mg) and **sulfur** (S): needed in large amounts (secondary nutrients)
- Iron, manganese, boron, zinc, copper, molybdenum and others: needed only in small amounts (**micronutrients**). Sugar beet rots inside the root when it lacks **boron**
- Rice takes up a lot of **silica** (Si), which hardens the leaves and makes the plant resistant to disease and lodging (Chapter 4)

### The price of fertilizer

- Japan imports almost all the raw materials of chemical fertilizers (phosphate rock, potassium ore, and urea made from natural gas). In 2022, world prices rose and fertilizer prices went up 1.5–2 times
- So there is growing interest in using domestic resources, such as **compost and fertilizer made from sewage sludge**, and in **variable-rate fertilizing**, which applies fertilizer only where it is needed (Chapter 12)

## Compost and green manure

![A pile of cow manure compost. It is fermented so the heat kills weed seeds and pathogens before it goes on the field](images/manure.jpg)

- **Compost**: livestock manure, straw, rice hulls, fallen leaves and the like, piled up and fermented
  - Increases organic matter, forms aggregates and increases microbes
  - Also contains nitrogen, phosphate and potassium (so you can use less chemical fertilizer)
  - If added before it has fully fermented, weed seeds, pathogens and sources of smell remain, and it can even damage crop roots
- **Green manure**: while a field is resting, grow oats, rye, hairy vetch (a legume), *Crotalaria* and so on, and plow them into the soil around flowering
  - Adds organic matter to the soil and prevents soil from washing away
  - Legume green manures add nitrogen
  - Some kinds reduce nematodes (for example, some oat varieties reduce the potato cyst nematode)
  - In Hokkaido, green manure is often sown in the summer after autumn-sown wheat is harvested

![Green manure (a cover crop). Grown while the field rests, then plowed into the soil](images/green-manure.jpg)

## Soil testing

- Every few years, take soil from the field and send it to JA, the prefectural experiment station or a fertilizer company, to measure pH, EC (salt concentration), phosphate, potassium, lime, magnesium, CEC and so on
- Look at the results and **add what is short and reduce what is too much**. If you spread the same fertilizer in the same amount for years, phosphate and potassium often build up too much in the soil
- Using less fertilizer saves money and also reduces runoff into rivers and groundwater (harm to the environment)
