## Soybeans

### Uses and numbers

- Japan uses about 3.5 million tonnes of soybeans a year. Almost all are imported and become **oil** (soybean oil) and the meal left after pressing out the oil (**soybean meal**, feed for cattle and pigs)
- Soybeans eaten as food (tofu, natto, miso, soy sauce, simmered beans) are about 1 million tonnes, and 20–30% of that is domestic
- Domestic soybeans are about 260,000 tonnes (2025 crop, 140,000 ha). Hokkaido grows about half, followed by Miyagi, Akita, Saga, Fukuoka, Shiga and others
- Domestic soybeans are popular for tofu and natto because they are not genetically modified and taste good

### The year of soybeans (Honshu example)

| Timing | Work and growth |
| --- | --- |
| June (late June–early July after wheat) | **Sowing**. Make ridges and sow on them. 5–7 kg per 10 a |
| June–July | Seedlings come up (cotyledons → unifoliate leaves → trifoliate leaves). **Inter-row cultivation and hilling** (cultivate between the ridges and pile soil around the base) once or twice |
| Late July–August | **Flowering**. The flowers are small and almost all self-pollinated |
| August–September | Pods form and the beans fill. The time when the plant needs the most water and nutrients |
| October | Leaves turn yellow and fall |
| Late October–November | **Harvest** (combine for beans). When the pods are completely dry and the beans rattle inside |

- Soybeans flower when they sense the days getting shorter (**short-day plants**). A northern variety grown in the south flowers too early, and a southern variety grown in the north meets winter before it flowers. Choose a variety suited to the region

### Root nodule bacteria

![Root nodules on soybean roots. Root nodule bacteria live inside and turn nitrogen from the air into a form plants can use](images/root-nodules.jpg)

- On the roots of soybeans and other legumes, small lumps called **root nodules** form. Bacteria called **rhizobia** (root nodule bacteria) live inside
- Rhizobia turn nitrogen (N₂) in the air into ammonia that the plant can use (**nitrogen fixation**). In return, they get sugar from the plant
- Thanks to this, soybeans grow with little nitrogen fertilizer, and nitrogen is left in the field after soybeans. This is a big reason to include beans in a rotation
- Root nodules need oxygen in the soil. They stop working when waterlogged (one reason waterlogging hits soybeans hard)
- Too much nitrogen fertilizer stops nodules from forming. Basal nitrogen for soybeans is kept small (2–3 kg per 10 a)

### The hard parts

- **Waterlogging**: fields that used to be paddies drain poorly, and if heavy rain falls after sowing, the seeds rot or do not come up. Drainage measures (Chapter 7) and sowing on high ridges are important
- **Weeds**: soybeans are slow to get going and easily lose out to summer weeds. They are held back with herbicides (after sowing and after emergence) and inter-row cultivation. Weeds that few herbicides can be used against in soybeans, such as introduced **morning glories**, are increasing
- **Insects**: the common cutworm, which eats leaves (western Japan), **stink bugs**, which suck the pods and beans (the beans become wrinkled), and the soybean pod borer (Hokkaido)
- **Diseases**: purple seed stain (purple marks on the beans), Phytophthora stem rot (wet fields), soybean cyst nematode
- **Harvest**: with much rain, the beans wrinkle or go moldy and the grade drops. If harvest is late, the pods burst and the beans fall

## Adzuki beans and kidney beans

![Adzuki beans. Used for sweet bean paste, Japanese sweets and *sekihan* (red rice)](images/adzuki.jpg)

- **Adzuki beans**: about 90% of domestic production is in Hokkaido (Tokachi). Used for sweet bean paste (*anko*), Japanese sweets, *sekihan* and *zenzai*. They are weak against cold and must be harvested before frost
- **Kidney beans** (*kintoki*, *tebō*, *ōfuku* and others): simmered beans and white bean paste. Almost all from Hokkaido
- Adzuki and kidney beans are crops whose harvest changes greatly with the weather, and whose prices go up and down easily. Imported beans (from China, Canada and elsewhere) carry high tariffs

## Buckwheat

![A field of buckwheat flowers. White flowers cover the field at the end of summer](images/buckwheat.jpg)

- Buckwheat is a plant of the knotweed family (Polygonaceae), not related to wheat or rice
- It can be harvested about **70–80 days** after sowing. It grows even on poor land and in cold places, so it has long been grown in mountain villages
  - Summer buckwheat (sown in spring) and **autumn buckwheat** (sown in July–August and harvested in October; this is more common)
- About 40% is grown in Hokkaido (Horokanai and elsewhere), followed by Nagano, Yamagata, Fukushima, Ibaraki, Tochigi and others
- The flowers are pollinated by insects (honeybees and others), so without bees the seeds fill poorly
- It falls over easily in rain and typhoons and is weak against frost. Yields are low, about 50–100 kg per 10 a, and change greatly with the weather
- Domestic buckwheat is 20–30% of consumption, and the rest is imported from China, the United States and elsewhere. It is often grown on former paddies as a conversion crop
