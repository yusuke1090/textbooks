# 2. How the rice plant grows

## The body of the rice plant

- Rice is a grass in the **grass family (Poaceae)**. Wheat, corn and barnyard millet are in the same family
- **Roots**: fibrous roots. So that it can breathe even in a flooded paddy, the stems and roots have air passages inside (aerenchyma)
- **Stems** (culms): they have nodes and are hollow inside. The very first stem is called the **main culm**
- **Leaves**: the leaf blade (the long, narrow part) and the leaf sheath that wraps the stem. On the main culm, about 13–17 leaves appear one after another, depending on the variety. The last leaf is called the **flag leaf**, and it is an important leaf for filling the ear
- **Ears** (panicles): appear at the tip of the stem. One ear carries about 80–120 grains

![Rice ears that have just headed. The white parts are stamens](images/rice-panicles.jpg)

## Stages of growth

The life of a rice plant is broadly divided into two parts.

| Stage | Timing (central Honshu, planted in May) | What happens |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Vegetative stage** | Transplanting to early July | Increases leaves and stems (tillers) and grows its body |
| **Reproductive stage** | Early July to heading | Makes the ear inside the stem (panicle initiation). The ear moves up inside the stem |
| **Ripening stage** | Heading to September | Sends starch made in the leaves to the grains, filling the rice |

### Leaf age

- Which leaf number has appeared on the main culm is called the **leaf age**. "4.5 leaves" means the 4th leaf is fully open and the 5th is halfway out
- Rice plants grow in step with their number of leaves, so deciding work by leaf age is more reliable than by calendar date (for example, seedlings are planted at 2.0–2.5 leaves; midseason drainage starts a few leaves before the flag leaf)
- One leaf takes about 5 days to appear in warm weather

### Tillering

- New stems branching out from the nodes at the base of a stem is called **tillering**. A hill that had 3–5 seedlings at transplanting can grow to 20 or more stems
- Not every tiller produces an ear. Thin stems that appear late end without heading (**unproductive tillers**)
- When the number of stems that will produce ears (**productive tillers**) is just right, the water is drained to stop more from forming (**midseason drainage**, Chapter 4). Too many stems make each ear smaller, and the plants fall over and get diseases more easily

### Panicle initiation stage

- About **25 days before heading**, a tiny ear (a **young panicle**) starts to form at the base of the stem. At first it is 1–2 mm
- Fertilizer added around this time is called **panicle fertilizer** (*hogoe*). It increases the number of grains and improves ripening (Chapter 4)
- **10–12 days before heading** is the **meiosis stage**, when pollen forms. If the temperature is low at this time (below 17 °C), pollen does not form properly, and the grains do not fill (**sterility**). This is **cold damage**

### Heading and flowering

![A rice flower. The grain opens and the stamens (white anthers) come out. It stays open for only about two hours in the morning](images/rice-flower.jpg)

- The ear coming out of the flag leaf's sheath is called **heading**. The day when half the stems in the field have headed is called the "heading date"
- On the day of heading, flowers open in turn from the tip of the ear. A rice flower is plain: the grain just pops open and the stamens come out
- Rice is almost always **pollinated by its own pollen** (self-pollination). So a variety's traits pass from parent to child unchanged
- If the temperature goes above 35 °C during flowering, pollination may fail

### Ripening (grain filling)

- Starch made in the leaves is carried to the pollinated grains, and the rice fills out
  - Milk stage (milky liquid inside) → dough stage (paste-like) → yellow-ripe stage (hard, grains turn yellow) → fully ripe stage
- A guide for harvest time is when the sum of daily average temperatures from heading (the **accumulated temperature**) reaches about 1,000 °C (about 40 days if summer averages 25 °C)
- The ears become heavy and bow their heads. A Japanese saying goes, "The more the rice ripens, the lower it bows its head"

## How yield is decided

Rice yield is decided by multiplying four numbers (the **yield components**).

> Yield = ears per m² × grains per ear × ripened grain ratio (share of grains that filled) × 1,000-grain weight (weight of 1,000 grains of brown rice)

A worked example (about right for Koshihikari):

| Component | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Ears per m² | 400 |
| Grains per ear | 80 → 32,000 grains per m² |
| Ripened grain ratio | 85% → 27,200 grains |
| 1,000-grain weight | 21 g → 27,200 ÷ 1,000 × 21 = 571 g |

- About 570 g per m² means about 570 kg per 10 a (1,000 m²) (about 9.5 bales)
- Each number is decided at a different time
  - Number of ears: the tillering period (transplanting to midseason drainage)
  - Number of grains: panicle initiation (panicle fertilizer)
  - Ripened grain ratio and 1,000-grain weight: after heading (weather, leaf health, water)
- The four are **in a trade-off**. Too many ears means fewer grains per ear and poorer filling. Farmers aim for a good balance

## Cold damage and heat damage

| Damage | When and why it happens | What happens | Countermeasures |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Sterility-type cold damage** | Below 17 °C at the meiosis stage (10–12 days before heading) | Pollen does not form and grains do not fill (sterility). In the great cold summer of 1993, the crop index was 74 | Deep water (raise the water to 15–20 cm to protect the young ear) |
| **Delayed-growth cold damage** | The whole summer is cool | Heading is late, and the grains do not finish ripening before the autumn cold | Fast-growing varieties, earlier transplanting |
| **Heat damage** | Average temperature above 26–27 °C for the 20 days after heading | The rice turns chalky (**chalky immature grains**) or cracks (fissured grains). The grade drops | Transplant later, heat-tolerant varieties, run water through to cool it, do not let fertilizer run out |

- Recently, **heat damage** has become a bigger problem than cold damage. In the extreme heat of 2023, the share of first-grade Koshihikari in Niigata dropped sharply
- So farmers are switching to heat-tolerant varieties (Niji no Kirameki, Tsuyahime, Shinnosuke, Kinumusume and others) and shifting their growing season
