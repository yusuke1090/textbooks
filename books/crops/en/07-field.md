## What is upland farming?

- **Upland farming** (*hatasaku*) means growing crops in fields that are not flooded. This textbook is not about vegetables or fruit (horticulture), but about **wheat, beans, potatoes, sugar beet, corn, buckwheat** and so on, grown with machines in wide fields
- Japan buys much of these from abroad

| Crop | Domestic production (approx.) | Self-sufficiency (approx.) | Hokkaido's share |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Wheat | About 1 million tonnes | About 15% | About 60–70% |
| Barley, naked barley | About 200,000 tonnes | About 10% | Small (Saga, Fukuoka, Tochigi and others) |
| Soybeans | About 260,000 tonnes (2025 crop) | About 7% (about 25% for soybeans eaten as food only) | About 50% |
| Potatoes | About 2.2 million tonnes | About 70% | About 80% |
| Sugar beet | About 3.5 million tonnes (root weight) | Sugar self-sufficiency is about 35% (including sugarcane) | All |
| Buckwheat | About 30,000–40,000 tonnes | About 20–30% | About 40% |

- Most wheat and soybeans are imported from the United States, Canada, Australia and Brazil. Imported wheat is bought in bulk by the government and sold to flour milling companies

## Field farming in Hokkaido

![The Tokachi Plain (near Obihiro) from the air. Large square fields divided by windbreaks](images/tokachi-aerial.jpg)

- Hokkaido, especially **Tokachi** (around Obihiro) and **Okhotsk** (around Abashiri and Kitami), is the center of Japan's field farming
- The average farm has about 40–50 ha of fields — more than ten times a Honshu rice farm. With large tractors and harvesters, the farming is close to that of Europe
- Summers are cool, with almost no rainy season. Winters are cold, and the soil freezes under snow and frost, so no crops can be grown in winter (except autumn-sown wheat)
- Between the fields stand **windbreaks** that protect the soil and crops from strong winds

### Rotating four crops

- The basis of field farming in Hokkaido is **crop rotation**: the **four main field crops** (wheat, sugar beet, potatoes and beans) are grown in turn, moving to a different field each year

| Year | Crop (example) |
| --- | --- |
| Year 1 | Autumn-sown wheat |
| Year 2 | Sugar beet |
| Year 3 | Beans (soybeans, adzuki beans, kidney beans) |
| Year 4 | Potatoes |
| Year 5 | Back to wheat |

- Benefits of rotation
  - Diseases and pests of the same crop do not build up (it prevents **continuous cropping injury**)
  - Root depths and the nutrients taken up differ, so the soil is used evenly
  - Beans put nitrogen from the air into the soil (Chapter 9)
  - After wheat, the field is free in summer, so green manure (Chapter 11) or compost can be added
  - Work comes at different times, so people and machines can be used efficiently all year
- Recently, sugar beet and potatoes, which take a lot of work, are decreasing, and "rotation breaking down" into mostly wheat and beans has become a problem

## Continuous cropping injury

If you keep growing the same crop (or crops of the same family) in the same field, growth gradually gets worse. This is called **continuous cropping injury**.

| Cause | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Pathogens in the soil increase | Sugar beet root rot, wheat take-all, soybean Phytophthora stem rot |
| Soil pests (nematodes) increase | The potato **cyst nematode** (once it gets in, it stays for decades; the government prevents its spread with quarantine) |
| Only certain nutrients run low | The same nutrients keep being taken up |
| Substances released by roots build up | They hinder the next planting of the same crop |

- Paddies (flooded rice) have almost no continuous cropping injury, because the water reduces pathogens and pests (Chapter 1). In upland farming, **crop rotation** is the best countermeasure

## Field farming in Honshu: wheat and soybeans grown in paddies

- In Honshu, Shikoku and Kyushu, much of the wheat and soybeans is grown in **paddies** that are not growing rice (converted paddies)
- Typical combinations
  - The **"three crops in two years"** of **rice → wheat → soybeans** (Kanto, Hokuriku, Tokai, Kyushu and others): rice in year 1, wheat sown in autumn and harvested in early summer the next year, then soybeans sown after it
  - **Paddy–upland rotation** (*denpata rinkan*): grow wheat and soybeans for a few years as an upland field, then turn it back into a paddy for rice. This reduces both upland continuous cropping injury and paddy weeds
- Paddies are built to hold water in the first place, so **drainage** is a big challenge when using them as upland fields. Neither wheat nor soybeans grow well when their roots sit in water (**waterlogging damage**)
  - **Open ditches** (*meikyo*): dig channels around and through the field to carry water to the outlet
  - **Underdrains** (*ankyo*): bury pipes underground to drain water. They can also be made cheaply with **mole drains** (a machine that makes round holes in the soil)
  - **Ridging** (*unetate*): make ridges and sow seed on top of them

## Subsidies for field farming

- Japanese wheat, soybeans, sugar beet and the like cost more to produce than cheap foreign products. So the government pays **grants** to fill the gap
  - **Direct payments for field crops** (the "*geta*" measure): paid according to the amount and quality of wheat, soybeans, sugar beet, potatoes for starch, buckwheat and rapeseed produced
  - **Measures to ease income decline** (the "*narashi*" measure): when income from rice, wheat, soybeans and so on falls well below a normal year, part of it is made up
- From fiscal 2027, the government plans to revise the grants for paddy conversion (Chapter 6) into a system that supports "farmers who raise yields of wheat, soybeans and feed crops, whether on paddies or upland fields". A form in which the grant grows the more a farmer harvests above average is being considered, and there are worries in Hokkaido that gaps between regions will widen
