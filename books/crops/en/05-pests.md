# 5. Diseases, pests and weeds

## What you will learn

- The most feared disease, "rice blast", and other main diseases
- Stink bugs that lower the grade of rice, and planthoppers that fly across the sea
- Apple snails
- Paddy weeds and herbicides

How often and how much pesticide to apply is decided from the local **spray calendar** (*bōjoreki*: a table from the prefecture or JA showing what to apply when) and **pest forecasts** (*hassei yosatsu*: forecasts of disease and insect outbreaks issued by the prefecture's plant protection office). Always follow the crop, amount, number of times and timing written on the pesticide label (the Agricultural Chemicals Regulation Act).

## Diseases

### Rice blast

![Rice blast. The nodes of the stem and the neck of the ear turn blackish brown, and the part beyond them dies](images/rice-blast.jpg)

- The disease that does the most damage to rice. It is spread by a fungus
- It has different names depending on where it appears
  - **Leaf blast**: spindle-shaped (pointed at both ends) brown spots on the leaves. When severe, the plants stop growing (stunting)
  - **Panicle blast** (neck blast): the neck or branches of the ear turn black, and the grains beyond do not fill (white heads). It directly hits the yield
- Conditions that favor it: **low temperatures and long rains** (the rainy season, cool summers), **too much nitrogen**, and leaves staying wet for a long time
- Countermeasures
  - Resistant varieties, disease-free seed and hot-water disinfection
  - Do not give too much nitrogen
  - Chemicals put into the trays (**nursery-box application**, spread on the seedling trays before transplanting) prevent leaf blast for a long time
  - For panicle blast, spray fungicide before and after heading
  - Spare seedlings left in the corner of the field for replanting are a source of disease, so clear them away early

### Other diseases

| Disease | Cause | Symptoms | Countermeasures |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sheath blight (*mongare*) | Fungus | Cloud-shaped patterns on the leaf sheaths at the base of the plant. Appears with summer heat and humidity and too many stems | Fungicide, do not increase stems too much |
| Bakanae disease | Fungus (on the seed) | Seedlings grow tall and spindly, then die | Seed disinfection |
| Rice stripe disease (*shimahagare*) | Virus (carried by the small brown planthopper) | Yellow stripes on the leaves, ears do not emerge | Resistant varieties, planthopper control |
| False smut (*ine-kōji*) | Fungus | Grains turn into green balls (smut balls) | Fungicide at the booting stage |

## Pests

### Stink bugs that cause pecky rice

![The southern green stink bug. When stink bugs suck juice from the grains, black spots appear on the rice](images/stink-bug.jpg)

- When stink bugs suck juice from grains that are still soft, the marks become brown-to-black **pecky rice** (*hantenmai*, spotted grains)
- It is harmless to eat, but if there is more than 1 in 1,000 grains of brown rice (0.1%), it becomes **second grade**, and above 0.3% third grade, and the price falls (Chapter 6)
- In Japanese paddies, small stink bugs such as the sorghum plant bug (*Stenotus rubrovittatus*) and the rice leaf bug (*Trigonotylus caelestialium*) are common. In warm regions, the southern green stink bug is also increasing
- Countermeasures
  - Stink bugs multiply on levees and surrounding grassland (in the ears of grassy weeds), and move into the paddy when the rice ears emerge. **Mow the levees by about two weeks before heading** to reduce their habitat (mowing just before heading drives them into the paddy instead, so be careful)
  - Insecticide 7–10 days after heading, and again about a week later
  - Reject black grains with an optical color sorter (Chapter 6)

### Planthoppers

![The brown planthopper. It sucks juice at the base of the plants and kills the rice in round patches (hopperburn)](images/planthopper.jpg)

- The **brown planthopper** and the white-backed planthopper cannot survive the winter in Japan. Every year, around the rainy season, they **fly in on the wind** from mainland China
- They multiply over several generations in the paddies by late summer, and when there are many brown planthoppers, rice whose stems have been sucked falls over and dies in round patches (**hopperburn**, *tsubogare*). There were large outbreaks in western Japan in 2019–2020
- The national and prefectural governments forecast when and how many will arrive. It is important to look at the base of the plants and count the insects
- The small brown planthopper survives the winter in Japan and carries the rice stripe virus

### Apple snails

![Eggs of the golden apple snail. Eye-catching pink, laid on canal walls and rice stems](images/snail-eggs.jpg)

- Their proper name is the **golden apple snail** (*Pomacea canaliculata*; *sukumiringogai*). A South American snail, it was imported for food in the 1980s, escaped, and spread to paddies in warm regions such as Kyushu, Shikoku and Kanto
- It eats the soft seedlings after transplanting
- Countermeasures
  - Plow the paddy in winter to break up snails wintering in the soil and expose them to the cold
  - Put nets on the canals to keep them out
  - For about three weeks after transplanting, keep the **water shallow** (4 cm or less) so the snails cannot move easily
  - Apply a chemical (granules that kill snails)
- A story once spread online that "they can be used for weeding", but they spread to other paddies, so the government asks people **not to release them on purpose**

## Weeds

![Barnyard grass (*Echinochloa*). While young, it looks just like rice and is hard to tell apart](images/barnyard-grass.jpg)

- Paddy weeds compete with rice for light, nutrients and water, and when there are many, yield drops sharply
- Typical paddy weeds

| Weed | Family | Features |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Barnyard grass** (*nobie*: *tainubie*, *inubie*) | Grass family | Looks just like rice. Does the most damage |
| **Monochoria** (*konagi*) | Pickerelweed family | Blue-purple flowers. Takes up a lot of nitrogen |
| Bulrush (*hotarui*) | Sedge family | Round stems. Types that herbicides do not work well on have appeared |
| Water chestnut (*kuroguwai*), arrowhead (*omodaka*) | Perennials (spread by bulb-like tubers) | Come back in the same place for years. Hard to eliminate |
| *Azena*, *kikashigusa* | Annual broadleaf weeds | Small and numerous |

![The flower of monochoria. A paddy weed, but the flower is pretty](images/monochoria.jpg)

- Herbicides
  - **Single-application herbicides**: applied once between right after transplanting and about two weeks later, they hold back many weeds for a long time. The main type today. They come as granules, flowables (liquid), "jumbo" packs (bags you throw in) and so on
  - After applying a herbicide, **keep the water still for about 7 days** (do not let it out or add more). A layer of chemical forms at the water's surface and works well, and it is kept from flowing into rivers
  - For weeds that remain, use products that work in the mid or late season
- If you keep using the same chemical, **weeds that herbicides do not work on** (resistance) appear. Combine several kinds
- Methods without pesticides: mechanical weeding (weeders and robots), deep water, spreading rice bran, planting through paper mulch and so on. They take effort, but the rice sells at a higher price as organic rice
