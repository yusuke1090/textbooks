# 3. Reading

## The Reading test

- **60 minutes** for **three long passages** (about 700–900 words each) and **40 questions**. The passages get harder as you go
- Academic passages come from books, magazines, newspapers and journals, written for a non-specialist reader. Topics vary: science, history, environment, psychology, technology and more
- On the paper test there is **no extra time** to transfer answers to the answer sheet. You must do it within the 60 minutes (unlike Listening)
- **Spelling mistakes make an answer wrong.** Capital or small letters do not matter
- Band 7 needs **about 30 correct answers out of 40** (it varies a little between tests)

## Question types

| Type | What you do | Order |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **TRUE / FALSE / NOT GIVEN** | Does the statement agree with the **facts** in the text? | Text order |
| **YES / NO / NOT GIVEN** | Does it agree with the writer's **views**? | Text order |
| **Matching headings** | Choose a heading for each paragraph | Not in order |
| **Matching information** | Which paragraph contains a piece of information? | Not in order |
| **Matching features** | Match people or things with descriptions | Not in order |
| **Multiple choice** | Choose from options | Text order |
| **Sentence / summary / note completion** | Fill gaps with words from the text | Usually text order |
| **Short-answer questions** | Answer briefly with words from the text | Text order |
| **Diagram label completion** | Complete labels on a diagram | Depends on the diagram |

- Gap-fill questions have **word limits** such as `NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS` or `ONE WORD AND/OR A NUMBER`. Going over the limit makes the answer wrong
- In gap-fill questions, copy **the words exactly as they appear** in the text. Do not change or paraphrase them
- If a summary completion gives a box of options (A–H and so on), write the **letter**, not the word

## TRUE / FALSE / NOT GIVEN

This is the type most people get wrong.

| Answer | When |
| --- | --- |
| **TRUE** | The text says the same thing (in other words) |
| **FALSE** | The text says **the opposite** |
| **NOT GIVEN** | The text has **no information** to decide whether it is true or false |

For example, if the text says "The farm keeps 120 cows, most of which are Holsteins.":

- The farm has more than 100 cows. → **TRUE** (120 is more than 100)
- All the cows on the farm are Holsteins. → **FALSE** (`most of which` means not all)
- The farm sells its milk to a local cheese maker. → **NOT GIVEN** (the text does not say where the milk goes)

- **Do not use your own knowledge**: even if something is true in real life, it is NOT GIVEN if the text does not say it
- If you cannot decide between FALSE and NOT GIVEN, look for a sentence that **clearly says the opposite**. If there is none, it is NOT GIVEN
- **Words of quantity or degree** such as `all`, `always`, `only`, `never`, `most` and `some` often decide the answer
- YES / NO / NOT GIVEN works the same way, but you compare with the **writer's opinion** (`argues`, `believes`, `suggests`) rather than facts

## Skimming and scanning

- **Skimming**: read quickly to see **what the text is about**. Read the first one or two sentences and the last sentence of each paragraph. Two to three minutes per passage
- **Scanning**: **search for specific words** such as names, numbers, years and technical terms. Move your eyes without reading every sentence
- The questions usually **paraphrase** the text (chapter 1). Look for the **same meaning**, not the same words
  - Question: `Farmers can sleep through the night.` → Text: `Producers are no longer woken before dawn.`
- When you find the right area, read **two or three sentences around it carefully** before deciding

## Using the 60 minutes

| Passage | Suggested time |
| --- | --- |
| 1 (easiest) | 17 minutes |
| 2 | 20 minutes |
| 3 (hardest) | 23 minutes |

1. Skim the passage to get the topic of each paragraph (2–3 minutes)
2. Read the questions and underline keywords (names, numbers and other words that are hard to paraphrase)
3. Find where the answer is, read carefully and answer
4. **Do not spend more than 2 minutes** on one question. Mark it, move on and come back at the end
5. **Never leave a blank.** Wrong answers lose no marks, so if time runs out, write something

- Doing **matching headings first** can show you how the passage is organised and make other questions easier
- On paper there is no transfer time, so copy your answers onto the answer sheet **after each passage**

## Practice passage

> **The milking robot**
>
> **A** For centuries, dairy farmers have woken before dawn to milk their cows, and then done the same again every evening, every day of the year. The first commercial milking robots, installed on farms in the Netherlands in the early 1990s, promised to end this routine. Today, tens of thousands of these machines operate around the world, and in some northern European countries a large proportion of new milking equipment is robotic.
>
> **B** A milking robot is not a humanoid machine but a stall containing a robotic arm. Instead of being brought in at fixed times, each cow decides for herself when to be milked. She walks into the stall, partly because she is given a small amount of concentrate feed there, and the robot identifies her from a tag on her collar. Cameras or lasers locate the four teats, the arm attaches the cups, and sensors record the volume and quality of the milk from each quarter of the udder. On average, cows choose to visit between two and three times a day, and one robot can usually serve about 60 cows.
>
> **C** The most obvious advantage is flexibility for the farmer. Since the robot works around the clock, farmers can organise their day around other tasks, or simply sleep. In addition, the data collected at every visit can reveal early signs of illness, such as mastitis, before a person would notice them. Some farmers also report that their cows produce more milk because they are milked more often.
>
> **D** However, the technology is not without problems. Each unit is expensive, and a farm with several hundred cows needs several robots. The machines also send alarms to the farmer's phone whenever something goes wrong, so night-time interruptions do not disappear entirely. Furthermore, cows that graze far from the barn may not return often enough to be milked, which makes the system harder to combine with grazing.
>
> **E** Despite these drawbacks, labour shortages in rural areas mean that interest in robotic milking is likely to keep growing. For many farming families, the machine offers something that no increase in milk price can buy: time.

- This passage was written for this book. Real test passages are three to four times longer
- First skim it for two minutes to get the topic of paragraphs A–E, then answer the questions below
- Exercises Q1–3 are TRUE / FALSE / NOT GIVEN; Q4–5 are gap fills
