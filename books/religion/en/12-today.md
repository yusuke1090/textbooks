# 12. Religion and today's world

## What you will learn in this chapter

- The relationship between states and religion (state religions, separation of religion and state, countries that restrict religion)
- How to look at conflicts involving religion
- What to be careful about regarding religion at work and when traveling

## States and religion

| Form | Contents | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A state religion is set | The constitution or law makes a particular religion the state religion or gives it a special place. Other religions are often recognized too | The UK (the Church of England), Denmark (Lutheran), Saudi Arabia and Iran (Islam), Malaysia (Islam), Cambodia (Buddhism) |
| Governed by religious law | Religious law (Islamic law and so on) is the basis of national law | Saudi Arabia, Iran, Afghanistan |
| Separation of religion and state | The state does not support a particular religion, and religion holds no political power | Japan, the United States, France, Turkey, India |
| Religion is restricted | The state manages religion and restricts its activities | China, North Korea |

- Separation of religion and state takes many forms
  - **United States**: there is no state religion, but religion is a strong force in society. Presidents take the oath of office with a hand on the Bible
  - **France**: "laïcité". It emphasizes keeping religion out of public life, and since 2004 has banned wearing conspicuous religious symbols (headscarves, large crosses, kippahs and so on) in public schools
  - **Japan**: Article 20 of the constitution sets out freedom of religion and that the state shall not engage in religious activity, and Article 89 that public money shall not be spent on religious organizations
  - **India**: the constitution makes it a secular state, but marriage and inheritance have separate laws for each religion

## Secularization, and movements that strengthen religion

- In Europe, North America and East Asia, the number of people who do not believe in religion is growing (secularization)
- Meanwhile, in Africa, the Middle East, South Asia and elsewhere, religion is still at the center of life and politics, and populations are growing fast, so worldwide three in four people still have a religion
- Movements trying to turn religion into political power (religious nationalism) are seen in various countries

## Conflicts involving religion

In fact, conflicts rarely arise from religious difference alone. Land, ethnicity, poverty and political power overlap, and religion often becomes a marker of "which side" someone is on.

| Region | Contents |
| --- | --- |
| Israel and Palestine | A conflict over land and statehood between Israel, a Jewish state, and the Arabs of Palestine (mostly Muslim, some Christian). Overlapping holy places in Jerusalem make it harder |
| Northern Ireland | A conflict between Catholics (many wanting unity with Ireland) and Protestants (many wanting to stay in the UK). The 1998 peace agreement ended the major fighting |
| India and Pakistan | The partition and independence of 1947, and the dispute over Kashmir |
| Myanmar | The Rohingya, a Muslim minority, were persecuted, and in 2017 more than 700,000 fled to neighboring Bangladesh |
| Nigeria, the Sahel | Attacks by Islamist extremist armed groups. Overlaps with conflicts between farmers and herders over land and water |
| The Middle East (Iraq, Syria and others) | Sunni–Shia rivalry overlaps with conflicts between states. From 2014, the extremist group IS controlled a wide area |

- Almost all believers of the religion concerned oppose extremist violence. It is important not to judge a whole religion by one incident
- On the other hand, religion is also a major force supporting society: hospitals, schools, help for the poor, mutual aid in disasters, mediation for peace and more

## What to be careful about at work and when traveling

### Food

| Religion | What to be careful about |
| --- | --- |
| Islam | Avoid pork and things made from pigs (lard, gelatin, bouillon) and alcohol (including mirin and sake used in cooking). Halal-certified restaurants and foods |
| Judaism | Kosher. Avoid pork, shrimp and shellfish; do not combine meat and dairy |
| Hinduism | Avoid beef. Many are vegetarian |
| Jainism | Besides meat, fish and eggs, some do not eat root vegetables |
| Buddhism | Monks and devout believers may avoid meat and alcohol (shojin ryori, temple cuisine) |
| Sikhism | Many avoid beef. Tobacco and alcohol are avoided |

- When inviting someone to a meal or choosing a restaurant, the surest way is to first ask, "Is there anything you can't eat?"
- Labels that clearly list ingredients, and vegetarian and halal marks, are a great help to visitors from abroad

### Prayer and days off

| Religion | What to be careful about |
| --- | --- |
| Islam | Prayer five times a day. When scheduling meetings and events, consider prayer times and a place (a quiet room). During Ramadan people do not eat in the daytime, so avoid lunch meetings and be considerate about eating and drinking in front of them. Friday noon is an important prayer |
| Judaism | From Friday sunset to Saturday sunset is the Sabbath. Some avoid work and travel |
| Christianity | Sunday is a day of worship and rest. Around Christmas and Easter, Western companies take long holidays |
| Hinduism | The Diwali season is a time of holidays and celebration for Indian companies |

### When entering religious buildings

- Wear clothes that do not show too much skin (cover shoulders and knees). In mosques, women are often asked to cover their hair with a scarf
- Take off your shoes in mosques, Hindu and Jain temples, gurdwaras and Japanese temple buildings. In a gurdwara, cover your head with a cloth
- Do not walk in front of people who are praying. Check whether photos are allowed
- In Thailand and elsewhere, do not touch people's heads (especially those of children and monks). Women do not touch monks

### When talking

- Do not assume someone's religion; if there is something you want to know, ask politely
- Do not make it the subject of jokes or teasing. Some people feel strongly about pictures or words depicting God or prophets
- Even within one religion, how rules are kept varies greatly by country, denomination and person. Do not think "they're Muslim, so they must do this"; adjust to the person

## The religious year (roughly)

| Time | Events |
| --- | --- |
| January | Orthodox Christmas (7th) |
| February–March | Lunar New Year in China, Korea and Vietnam; Holi |
| March–April | Easter, Passover |
| May | Vesak (Buddha's Day) |
| August | Obon in Japan |
| September–October | Jewish New Year and Yom Kippur, Navaratri |
| October–November | Diwali |
| December | Christmas, Hanukkah |
| Shifts every year | Ramadan and Eid (on the Islamic calendar, they come about 11 days earlier each year; Ramadan in 2027 is around February) |

## Summary

- The relationship between state and religion varies: state religions, religious law, separation of religion and state, restriction of religion
- Most conflicts arise where land, ethnicity and politics overlap with religion. Do not judge a whole religion by one incident
- Know the rules on food, prayer, days off and dress, and when unsure, ask the person
