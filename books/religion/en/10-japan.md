# 10. Religion in Japan

## Japanese religion in numbers

| Survey | Figures |
| --- | --- |
| Believers in the Agency for Cultural Affairs' "Religion Yearbook" (2022 edition) | Shinto-related about 87 million, Buddhist about 83 million, Christian about 1.9 million, others about 7.1 million. About 180 million in total, more than the population |
| Pew survey (2023, adults) | Buddhist 46%, Shinto 4%, no religion 42% |
| Pew estimate (2020, including children) | No religion 57% (about 73 million) |

- The "Religion Yearbook" figures are self-reported by shrines and temples. Shrines count local residents (ujiko) and temples count their danka families, so one person is counted in both, giving more than the population
- Even people who say "I don't believe in religion" mostly take part in New Year shrine visits, Obon, grave visits and amulets as customs. This is because in Japan "religion" is easily taken to mean joining a particular religious organization
- There are about 180,000 religious organizations nationwide: about 84,000 Shinto-related and about 77,000 Buddhist (the "Religion Yearbook", 2024 edition)

## Shinto

### Overview

| Item | Details |
| --- | --- |
| Gods | The eight million kami (yaoyorozu no kami). Nature (mountains, rivers, trees, rocks, the sea), ancestors and outstanding people are also enshrined as kami |
| Founder, scripture | None. There is no fixed scripture. The myths are written in the "Kojiki" (712) and the "Nihon Shoki" (720) |
| Places of worship | Shrines. A torii gate marks the entrance. At home, a kamidana (household shrine shelf) |
| Clergy | Shinto priests (guji, negi). Shrine maidens (miko) assist |
| Central shrine | Ise Grand Shrine (enshrining Amaterasu Omikami) |

![The purification fountain at Ise Grand Shrine (Mie Prefecture). Before praying, visitors purify their hands and mouth here](images/ise.jpg)

### Ideas

- **Impurity and purification**: Shinto dislikes the impurity of death, illness and the like, and values a pure state, purifying the hands and mouth with water (temizu) and receiving purification rites (oharai)
- **Matsuri**: welcoming, entertaining and thanking the kami, and praying for good harvests and safety. Most local festivals are shrine festivals
- **Gratitude to nature**: sacred ropes (shimenawa) are tied around large trees and rocks, which are treasured as places where kami dwell
- There is little detailed teaching about what happens after death or the right way to live; it is woven into daily life and seasonal events

## Japanese Buddhism

### History

| Period | Event |
| --- | --- |
| 6th century | Buddhism arrives from Baekje on the Korean Peninsula (538 and 552 are both given). The Soga and Mononobe clans fight over whether to accept it |
| Asuka period | Prince Shotoku values Buddhism and builds Horyuji and other temples |
| Nara period | Buddhism to protect the nation. Emperor Shomu builds provincial temples (kokubunji) in each province and makes the Great Buddha of Todaiji (consecrated in 752) |
| Heian period | Saicho founds the Tendai school (Mount Hiei), and Kukai the Shingon school (Mount Koya) |
| Kamakura period | New schools easy for warriors and common people to understand arise (table below) |
| Edo period | To suppress Christianity, the "terauke system" requires every household to belong to some temple. The "danka system" forms, in which temples handle funerals and memorial services for ancestors |
| Meiji period | With the separation of Shinto and Buddhism, Buddha images and temple buildings were sometimes destroyed (haibutsu kishaku) |

![Horyuji (Nara Prefecture). Built in the 7th century; among the oldest wooden buildings in the world](images/horyuji.jpg)

![The Great Buddha of Todaiji (Nara Prefecture)](images/todaiji.jpg)

### Main schools

| School | Founder | Characteristics |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tendai | Saicho | Studies many teachings, centered on the Lotus Sutra. Many founders of the Kamakura schools came from Mount Hiei |
| Shingon | Kukai | Esoteric Buddhism. Mantras and rituals. Mount Koya. The Shikoku pilgrimage of 88 temples |
| Jodo (Pure Land) | Honen | By reciting "Namu Amida Butsu", one can be reborn in Amida Buddha's Pure Land paradise |
| Jodo Shinshu (True Pure Land) | Shinran | Values faith in Amida Buddha itself. Monks also married. Today the school with the most followers (Nishi Honganji, Higashi Honganji and others) |
| Rinzai | Eisai | Zen. Contemplates questions (koan) set by the teacher. Spread among warriors and linked to the culture of the tea ceremony and gardens |
| Soto | Dogen | Zen. Just sitting (shikantaza). The school with the most temples |
| Nichiren | Nichiren | Values the Lotus Sutra above all, and recites "Namu Myoho Renge Kyo" |

### Buddhism in daily life

- Most funerals are held in Buddhist form. The deceased is given a posthumous Buddhist name (kaimyo or homyo), and memorial services are held on the 49th day, the first anniversary and so on
- Memorial tablets (ihai) are placed in the household Buddhist altar (butsudan) to honor ancestors
- In rural areas the number of danka families is falling, and more temples have no resident priest

## Shinbutsu-shugo and the Meiji separation

- From the Nara period, "shinbutsu-shugo", worshipping kami and buddhas together, developed. It was thought that "Japan's kami are buddhas who appeared in different forms to save people" (honji suijaku), and it was common for a temple to stand within a shrine
- In 1868, the Meiji government issued orders separating Shinto and Buddhism, dividing shrines from temples. Shinto then became "State Shinto", managed by the state, and was tied to nation-building centered on the emperor and to mobilization for war
- After defeat in 1945, the state and Shinto were separated, and the Constitution of Japan established freedom of religion and the separation of religion and state (Articles 20 and 89)

## Christianity in Japan

- It arrived in the 16th century and, after an age of prohibition and hiding, was recognized again in the Meiji era (Chapter 4)
- Believers are about 1% of the population, but it is familiar through mission schools and hospitals, church weddings, Christmas and more
- The heritage of the hidden Christians of Nagasaki and Amakusa became a World Heritage Site in 2018

![Oura Cathedral (Nagasaki), where hidden Christians revealed their faith in 1865 (the "discovery of believers")](images/oura.jpg)

## New religions

| Period | Main groups |
| --- | --- |
| Late Edo to Meiji | Tenrikyo (1838), Konkokyo, Kurozumikyo, Oomoto |
| Showa | Soka Gakkai (based on Nichiren's teachings; 1930), Rissho Kosei-kai, Reiyukai, Seicho-no-Ie, PL Kyodan and others |
| Late Showa onward | Groups called "new new religions". Some emphasize self-development or mystical experience |

- Some groups have become social problems. Aum Shinrikyo carried out the Tokyo subway sarin attack in 1995 and was dissolved by court order
- The former Unification Church (the Family Federation for World Peace and Unification) became an issue over harm caused by huge donations; the Tokyo District Court ordered its dissolution in March 2025, and this was finalized by the Supreme Court in June 2026

## The events of the year and life milestones

| Event | When | Religious origin | Contents |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Hatsumode | New Year | Shinto, Buddhism | Visiting a shrine or temple to pray for a safe year |
| Setsubun | Around February 3 | Shinto, Buddhism, folk | Throwing beans to drive away demons (misfortune) |
| Higan | March, September | Buddhism | Grave visits around the spring and autumn equinoxes |
| Hana Matsuri | April 8 | Buddhism | The Buddha's birthday |
| Obon | August (July in some regions) | Buddhism, ancestral rites | Welcoming and sending off ancestral spirits. Bon dances, farewell fires |
| Shichi-Go-San | November | Shinto | Celebrating the growth of children aged 3, 5 and 7 |
| Christmas | December 25 | Christianity | Spread as an event for families and couples rather than as a religious occasion |
| New Year's Eve | December 31 | Buddhism | Temple bells are rung 108 times (joya no kane) |

![Bon odori (the Obon dance)](images/bon-odori.jpg)

- At life milestones, it is common to take a newborn to a shrine (omiyamairi), have a Christian-style or Shinto wedding, and a Buddhist funeral. This is sometimes called "picking the best of each", but dividing roles by occasion has long been a feature of Japanese religion

## Summary

- Many people say they "don't believe in religion", but Shinto and Buddhism are deeply part of life as events and customs
- Shinto: the eight million kami, impurity and purification, matsuri. Shrines and kamidana
- Buddhism: arrived in the 6th century, with many schools in the Kamakura period. Through the Edo-period danka system, it took on the role of funerals and ancestor memorials
- Shinbutsu-shugo lasted a long time and was separated in the Meiji era. Separation of religion and state after the war
