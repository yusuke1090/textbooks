# 3. 2×2 square matrices and linear transformations

Using 2×2 matrices, we first get a taste of the key ideas of linear algebra (inverses, determinants, transformations, eigenvalues).

## The inverse matrix

A matrix $X$ with $AX = XA = E$ is called the **inverse** of $A$ and is written $A^{-1}$. It corresponds to the "reciprocal" of a number.

For 2×2 square matrices there is a formula.

$$A = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix},\quad \text{when } \Delta = ad - bc \ne 0, \quad A^{-1} = \frac{1}{ad - bc}\begin{pmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{pmatrix}$$

- **When $ad - bc = 0$, there is no inverse** (the matrix is not invertible)
- $ad - bc$ is called the **determinant** of $A$ and is written $\det A$ or $\lvert A \rvert$ (more in Chapter 5)
- How to remember: "**swap the diagonal, change the signs of the other diagonal, and divide by $ad - bc$**"

### Example 3-1

Find the inverse of $A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 2 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and check that $AA^{-1} = E$.

**Solution** Since $\det A = 3 \cdot 1 - 2 \cdot 1 = 1 \ne 0$,

$$A^{-1} = \frac{1}{1}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -2 \\ -1 & 3 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -2 \\ -1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$$

$$AA^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 - 2 & -6 + 6 \\ 1 - 1 & -2 + 3 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = E$$

### Solving a system with the inverse

Multiplying both sides of $A\boldsymbol{x} = \boldsymbol{b}$ by $A^{-1}$ from the left gives $\boldsymbol{x} = A^{-1}\boldsymbol{b}$.

### Example 3-2

Solve $\begin{cases} 3x + 2y = 7 \\ x + y = 3 \end{cases}$ using the inverse.

**Solution** Using $A^{-1}$ from Example 3-1,

$$\begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -2 \\ -1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 7 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 7 - 6 \\ -7 + 9 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}$$

$x = 1$, $y = 2$.

### Properties of the inverse

- $(A^{-1})^{-1} = A$
- $(AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1}$ (**the order is reversed**: if you put on socks and then shoes, you take off the shoes first)
- $({}^tA)^{-1} = {}^t(A^{-1})$

## The Cayley–Hamilton theorem

For $A = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}$, the following always holds.

$$A^2 - (a + d)A + (ad - bc)E = O$$

$a + d$ (the sum of the diagonal entries) is called the **trace** of $A$ ($\text{tr}\,A$).

### Example 3-3

Check that the Cayley–Hamilton theorem holds for $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$.

**Solution** $A^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 7 & 10 \\ 15 & 22 \end{pmatrix}$, $\text{tr}\,A = 5$ and $\det A = 4 - 6 = -2$, so

$$A^2 - 5A - 2E = \begin{pmatrix} 7 - 5 - 2 & 10 - 10 - 0 \\ 15 - 15 - 0 & 22 - 20 - 2 \end{pmatrix} = O$$

### Finding $A^n$

The Cayley–Hamilton theorem lets you calculate $A^n$. Find the **remainder** $px + q$ when $x^n$ is divided by $x^2 - (a+d)x + (ad - bc)$; then $A^n = pA + qE$ (because putting $A$ into the divisor gives $O$).

### Example 3-4

When $A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$, find $A^n$.

**Solution** $\text{tr}\,A = 5$ and $\det A = 6$, so $A^2 - 5A + 6E = O$.

Let $Q(x)$ be the quotient and $px + q$ the remainder when $x^n$ is divided by $x^2 - 5x + 6 = (x - 2)(x - 3)$:

$$x^n = (x - 2)(x - 3)Q(x) + px + q$$

Putting in $x = 2$ and $x = 3$ gives $2^n = 2p + q$ and $3^n = 3p + q$. Solving,

$$p = 3^n - 2^n, \qquad q = 3 \cdot 2^n - 2 \cdot 3^n$$

Therefore

$$A^n = pA + qE = \begin{pmatrix} 3p + q & p \\ 0 & 2p + q \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 3^n & 3^n - 2^n \\ 0 & 2^n \end{pmatrix}$$

Check: for $n = 2$ this gives $\begin{pmatrix} 9 & 5 \\ 0 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$, which matches $A^2$ calculated directly.

> **Key point**: The $2$ and $3$ that appeared here are the **eigenvalues** you will learn in Chapter 8. $A^n$ can also be found by **diagonalization** in Chapter 8.

## Linear transformations

A transformation that moves a point $(x, y)$ to a point $(x', y')$ by multiplying by a matrix $A$ is called a **linear transformation**.

$$\begin{pmatrix} x' \\ y' \end{pmatrix} = A\begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix}$$

### Typical linear transformations

| Transformation | Matrix |
| --- | --- |
| Rotation by $\theta$ about the origin | $\begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix}$ |
| $a$ times in the $x$ direction, $b$ times in the $y$ direction | $\begin{pmatrix} a & 0 \\ 0 & b \end{pmatrix}$ |
| Reflection in the line $y = x$ | $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ |
| Reflection in the $x$ axis | $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$ |

> **Key point**: The matrix of a linear transformation has "where $\boldsymbol{e}_1 = (1, 0)$ goes" as its first column and "where $\boldsymbol{e}_2 = (0, 1)$ goes" as its second column. The rotation matrix, too, can be made at once from the fact that $(1, 0)$ goes to $(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$ and $(0, 1)$ goes to $(-\sin\theta, \cos\theta)$.

### Example 3-5

Find the point you get by rotating the point $(2, 0)$ by $60°$ about the origin.

**Solution**

$$\begin{pmatrix} \cos 60° & -\sin 60° \\ \sin 60° & \cos 60° \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \cdot \frac{1}{2} \\ 2 \cdot \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ \sqrt{3} \end{pmatrix}$$

### A composite transformation is a matrix product

If you do transformation $f$ (matrix $A$) and then transformation $g$ (matrix $B$), the matrix of the combined transformation is **$BA$** (the one done later goes on the **left**).

### Example 3-6

From the fact that a rotation by $\alpha$ followed by a rotation by $\beta$ is a rotation by $(\alpha + \beta)$, derive the addition formulas of trigonometry.

**Solution** Since $R(\beta)R(\alpha) = R(\alpha + \beta)$,

$$\begin{pmatrix} \cos\beta & -\sin\beta \\ \sin\beta & \cos\beta \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} \cos\alpha & -\sin\alpha \\ \sin\alpha & \cos\alpha \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \cos(\alpha+\beta) & -\sin(\alpha+\beta) \\ \sin(\alpha+\beta) & \cos(\alpha+\beta) \end{pmatrix}$$

Calculating and comparing the (1, 1) and (2, 1) entries of the left side,

$$\cos(\alpha + \beta) = \cos\alpha\cos\beta - \sin\alpha\sin\beta, \qquad \sin(\alpha + \beta) = \sin\alpha\cos\beta + \cos\alpha\sin\beta$$

The addition formulas you memorized in high school come out naturally from a matrix product.

### The area scale factor is $\lvert\det A\rvert$

![With A = (2 1; 1 2), a square of area 1 goes to a parallelogram of area 3](images/en/transform.svg)

- Under a linear transformation, the area of a figure is multiplied by $\lvert\det A\rvert$. $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ in the figure above has $\det A = 4 - 1 = 3$, so the area triples
- A matrix with $\det A = 0$ **squashes** the plane **onto a line (or a point)**. What has been squashed cannot be restored, which is why there is no inverse
- When $\det A < 0$, the figure is **flipped over** (reflections and so on)

## Exercises

1. Find the inverse of $\begin{pmatrix} 4 & 3 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$.
2. Find $k$ so that $\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 4 & k \end{pmatrix}$ has no inverse.
3. When $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$, find $A^3$ using the Cayley–Hamilton theorem.
4. Find the matrix of the transformation that reflects in the line $y = x$ and then rotates by $90°$ about the origin.

## Answers to the exercises

1. $\det = 4 - 3 = 1$, so $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -3 \\ -1 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$
2. From $2k - 4 = 0$, $k = 2$
3. From $A^2 - 4A + 3E = O$, $A^2 = 4A - 3E$. $A^3 = 4A^2 - 3A = 4(4A - 3E) - 3A = 13A - 12E = \begin{pmatrix} 14 & 13 \\ 13 & 14 \end{pmatrix}$
4. The rotation done later goes on the left, so $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ (reflection in the $y$ axis)
