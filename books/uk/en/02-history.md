## Ancient times: Stonehenge and Rome

![Stonehenge (Wiltshire, southern England). Each large stone weighs about 25 tonnes](images/stonehenge.jpg)

- At Stonehenge, the stones line up with the sunrise on the summer solstice and the sunset on the winter solstice. It is thought to have been a place for calendars and festivals. The smaller stones (bluestones) were brought from mountains in Wales about 240 km away
- The Romans built roads, towns and baths. London (Londinium) and Bath (a hot-spring town) date from this time
- They could not conquer Scotland, so Emperor Hadrian built a wall about 120 km long from east to west

![Hadrian's Wall. Today you can walk along it as a footpath](images/hadrians-wall.jpg)

## 1066: the Norman Conquest and English

![The Bayeux Tapestry (an embroidered picture scroll). The scene where King Harold of England falls at the Battle of Hastings in 1066](images/bayeux.jpg)

- William, Duke of Normandy (the Conqueror), won, and the king and nobles began speaking French. The farmers kept speaking Old English (the language of the Anglo-Saxons)
- Because of this, English has **many cases where the same thing has two words**. The most famous is that **living animals have English names, while their meat has French names**

| Animal (the word of the farmers who raised it) | Meat (the word of the nobles who ate it) |
| --- | --- |
| cow | beef (← French *bœuf*) |
| pig / swine | pork (← *porc*) |
| sheep | mutton (← *mouton*) |
| calf | veal (← *veau*) |

- In 1086, William had all the land, farmers and livestock in the country surveyed and compiled in the "Domesday Book". It is the oldest survey of an entire country's agriculture in Europe

## Parliament grows stronger

![Magna Carta (1215). Copies survive today in the British Library and elsewhere](images/magna-carta.jpg)

- **Magna Carta** (the Great Charter): a document in which the barons made King John promise not to take taxes or arrest people as he pleased. It was the beginning of the idea that "even the king must obey the law"
- After that, the system that Parliament's consent is needed to set taxes gradually developed
- In the 17th century, the king and Parliament fought, and after the civil war, the **Glorious Revolution** (1688, in which almost no blood was shed) made Parliament the center of the state. "The king reigns but does not rule"
- This is the basis of today's **constitutional monarchy** and parliamentary politics. Japan's Meiji constitution and parliament also took Britain as one of their models

## The Agricultural and Industrial Revolutions

- The **Agricultural Revolution** of the 18th century
  - **Enclosure**: long narrow strips and common land used by the whole village were enclosed with hedges and stone walls to make large fields for single landowners. Production rose, but farmers who lost their land moved to the towns. Today's British landscape of "fields divided by hedges" was formed at this time (Chapter 6)
  - **The Norfolk four-course system**: a four-year rotation of wheat → turnips → barley → clover. It did away with fallow years and, thanks to turnips and clover, let farmers keep livestock through the winter. Livestock manure fertilized the fields
  - Selective breeding: Robert Bakewell selected and crossed sheep and cattle to create livestock with more meat. The beginning of many of today's livestock breeds
- People who moved to the towns worked in factories, and the **Industrial Revolution** began
  - Cotton thread and cloth made in large quantities by machine
  - Watt's steam engine (patented in 1769), railways (the world's first public railway in 1825)
  - Coal and iron. In 1779, the world's first iron bridge (the Iron Bridge) was built

![The Iron Bridge (1779). The world's first iron bridge, and a World Heritage Site](images/iron-bridge.jpg)

## The British Empire

- In the 19th century, Britain ruled about a quarter of the world's land (India, Canada, Australia, New Zealand, many countries in Africa and others). "The empire on which the sun never sets"
- Even today there is a group of former colonies (the **Commonwealth**, 56 countries). Kenya, Tanzania and Malawi are members too (see the Africa Textbook)
- In 1846, the **Corn Laws** (laws that put high taxes on cheap foreign wheat to protect landowners) were repealed, and Britain became a free-trade country, buying cheap food from abroad and selling industrial goods. That is why Britain still buys much of its food from abroad (Chapter 6)

## After the two world wars

- In the Second World War, German submarines sank ships carrying food, and food ran short. Under the slogan "**Dig for Victory**", even parks and gardens were turned into fields
- So after the war, the government guaranteed prices for farm products and put effort into growing food at home (the Agriculture Act of 1947)
- The **NHS** (National Health Service) began in 1948. It is still funded by taxes, and treatment is almost free. It is a source of pride for British people, and at the same time, long waiting times are always a problem
- India and other colonies became independent one after another, and the empire came to an end
- In 1973 Britain joined the EC (today's EU). Farming came under the EU's Common Agricultural Policy (**CAP**), with subsidies paid according to land area

## Leaving the EU (Brexit)

- In the 2016 referendum, leaving was decided by 52% to 48%
  - Reasons for voting to leave: wanting to decide immigration numbers themselves, not wanting to be bound by EU rules, the money paid to the EU
  - Reasons for voting to remain: trade, jobs, young people's study and travel abroad
  - London, Scotland and Northern Ireland mostly voted to remain; rural England and Wales mostly voted to leave
- Britain left on January 31, 2020, and from January 2021 trade with the EU required **checks and paperwork**. Food trade in particular — meat, dairy products, plants and so on — became more troublesome
- In farming, instead of EU subsidies (CAP), England is moving to a system that "pays only for what you do that is good for the environment" (Chapter 6)
- To avoid border checks on the island of Ireland, Northern Ireland received special treatment: for goods, it follows some EU rules

## From Queen to King

![The procession at the coronation of Charles III (May 2023, London)](images/charles3-coronation.jpg)

- Elizabeth II was queen for 70 years, from 1952 to 2022. She died in September 2022, and her eldest son Charles III became king. The coronation was in May 2023
- Since he was Prince of Wales, Charles III has been very keen on organic farming and the environment, and has farmed organically on his own estate (Highgrove)

## Links with Japan

- In 1863, five young men from the Chōshū domain (including Itō Hirobumi and Inoue Kaoru — the **Chōshū Five**) secretly went to study in London. They later became central figures in Japan's government, railways and mint
- Japan's first railway (Shimbashi–Yokohama), opened in 1872, was built with British engineers, locomotives and money
- The **Anglo-Japanese Alliance** was signed in 1902 (lasting until 1923)
- Japan and Britain are both island nations next to a continent, with a royal (imperial) family and driving on the left. They have a lot in common, and many people in each country feel a friendly closeness to the other
- Today many Japanese companies operate there, such as Hitachi's railway trains (Britain's intercity express trains) and Nissan's Sunderland factory (Chapter 5)
